# Multi-Layer Layout System

## Purpose
A 3×3 collage is a nine-tile editorial composition built from layered photography. Each tile may contain several different photographs; it must not resemble nine isolated single-photo cards or a neat arrangement of rectangles on a blank sheet.

## Art direction
Use clean neutral/cool near-white, black/ink, and restrained red. Match the background to pale sky, snow, mist, and grey-white source-photo edges. Avoid yellowed paper, beige vintage textures, heavy paper grain, oversaturated poster color, and large empty gutters.

Preserve subdued natural source colors. Apply modest selective desaturation or contrast reduction to support/ghost layers only when useful; do not force the whole collage into grayscale. Keep some authentic blue, grey-blue, earth tones, and flag colors visible.

## Tile layer model
Plan each tile as a stack, using only layers that improve the composition:
1. Background: clean neutral/cool near-white matched to source edges.
2. Underlay/ghost photo: atmosphere, shape, or texture; can be muted and partly hidden.
3. Secondary photo/detail: a distinct source image, crop, strip, or silhouette.
4. Hero photo: dominant recognizable subject, stronger detail and more natural color.
5. Optional additional photo fragment.
6. Illustration or extracted cutout.
7. Line art: Gesang flowers, yak contours, mountain ridges, roads, architecture, prayer-flag cords, or relevant outlines.
8. Typography: accurate editable type; Tibetan text verified and restrained, Chinese optional.
9. Color-extension layer: limited red marks or black shapes.
10. Cross-grid layer: selected photos, text, lines, or motifs placed on the full canvas and sliced afterward.

Do not force every layer into every tile. For a multi-photo collage, make at least two distinct photo layers visibly present in most tiles. One layer should partially cover another; hidden portions are intentional occlusion.

## Overlap and masking
- Let photos overlap and cover one another; do not simply arrange intact rectangles side-by-side.
- Use irregular masks, crops, cutouts, partial fades, and controlled opacity to establish foreground/background order.
- Keep underlays visible enough to read as image shapes, not just dirty texture.
- Vary z-order and overlap patterns per tile.
- Avoid repeated white brush borders. Match any painted/torn edge to the adjacent sky/snow/mist tone, or use soft mask transitions and layered imagery.
- Do not desaturate every photo. Hero layers can retain natural muted color; support layers can be gently desaturated; ghost layers can be more subdued.

## Grid roles
- Center: visually substantive anchor or photomontage, not automatically a blank title card. Avoid oversized sharp Tibetan lettering.
- Corners: establish tone and frame the composition.
- Edge tiles: may host motifs/text that point inward or cross a boundary.
- Middle-edge tiles: useful for bridges between neighboring compositions.
- Diagonals: can create a secondary path through the whole grid.

## Shared cross-tile layers
Plan the full 3×3 canvas in one coordinate system before slicing into nine squares. A shared layer can be a photograph, Tibetan text, line-art flower, mountain ridge, road, prayer-flag cord, or restrained red graphic.

Example: one landscape photo may span the right half of tile A and left half of tile B. Place/crop it once on the full canvas, then slice at the tile boundaries. Do not resize the same photo separately inside A and B. Shared layers can bridge several boundaries, but most tiles should still have distinct photo combinations.

## Density and whitespace
Photography should occupy most of each tile. Keep outer margins and gutters narrow; use whitespace around irregular photo edges and deliberate breathing spaces, not as a large blank frame. Vary density; no tile should feel accidentally empty. Keep motifs controlled and varied. Tibetan script is not a repeated decoration: use verified text sparingly in different sizes/placements.

## Deliverables
Before rendering, provide a tile plan listing each tile's distinct photo candidates, layer order, masks/tone adjustments, text, motifs, and shared layers crossing boundaries. Render a low-resolution proof and review the full grid at phone size.
