# Connection Rules

This document defines how adjacent photographs can form a visual connection.

The goal is not to make two images look identical. The goal is to make the viewer's eye naturally move from one tile into the next.

## 1. Connection Types

Prefer real visual relationships over artificial decoration.

### 1.1 Direction Continuity

A strong line or movement in one image points toward the neighboring image.

Examples:

- road → road
- river → river
- mountain ridge → mountain ridge
- person walking → direction of the next image
- cloud movement → cloud movement

This is one of the strongest connection types.

### 1.2 Shape Continuity

A shape in one image resembles or continues a shape in the neighboring image.

Examples:

- mountain ridge → roofline
- waterfall edge → river curve
- flower stem → grass line
- yak horn → mountain silhouette

The shapes do not need to be literally connected.

### 1.3 Color Continuity

A dominant color in one tile is echoed by the neighboring tile.

Examples:

- red prayer flags → red clothing
- blue sky → blue water
- green grass → green hillside
- white snow → white cloud

Color continuity is a supporting connection, not a substitute for composition.

### 1.4 Object Continuity

The same object, or a logically related object, appears across neighboring frames.

Examples:

- prayer flag → prayer flag
- road sign → road
- temple roof → temple wall
- yak → grassland

Do not invent an object merely to create continuity.

### 1.5 Texture Continuity

Similar textures can bridge two photographs.

Examples:

- grass → grass
- rock → mountain
- snow → cloud
- water surface → mist

Texture continuity works especially well when the boundary is subtle.

### 1.6 Spatial Continuity

The spatial position of an element helps the two images feel like parts of the same environment.

Examples:

- foreground object on the right → open space on the left of the next image
- mountain occupying the upper-right → mountain continuing from the upper-left
- road leaving the frame → road entering the next frame

### 1.7 Narrative Continuity

Two photographs can connect because the second image feels like a continuation of the first.

Examples:

- road → destination
- river → waterfall
- grassland → grazing yak
- temple exterior → prayer flags
- person looking → landscape being observed

Narrative continuity should support visual continuity rather than replace it.

---

## 2. Connection Strength

Use a simple 0–5 score for each connection.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 5 | Very strong and immediately visible |
| 4 | Strong connection |
| 3 | Clear but subtle |
| 2 | Weak supporting relationship |
| 1 | Barely related |
| 0 | No useful connection |

A connection can combine several dimensions.

Example:

```text
A → B

direction: 5
shape:     4
color:     4
object:    2
texture:   3
space:     5
narrative: 4

overall: strong
```

The exact mathematical average is optional. The important principle is to compare candidate arrangements consistently.

---

## 3. Strong vs Weak Connections

### Strong

A viewer notices the relationship without being told.

Examples:

- a road exits the right edge of one image and a road enters from the left edge of the next
- a mountain ridge has a similar slope across the boundary
- a river visually continues into the neighboring photograph
- a red prayer flag cluster is echoed by another red element

### Weak

The relationship exists only because the designer explains it.

Examples:

- both photographs happen to contain green
- both contain mountains but with unrelated compositions
- both were taken in the same region
- both contain sky but the sky does not visually connect

Weak connections can support a layout, but should not carry the main composition.

---

## 4. Boundary Thinking

Always inspect the actual shared boundary.

For horizontal neighbors:

```text
[A | B]
  ↑
inspect the right edge of A
and the left edge of B
```

For vertical neighbors:

```text
[A]
---
[B]

inspect the bottom edge of A
and the top edge of B
```

Ask:

> If the grid line disappeared, would these two images feel more naturally related?

This is the central test.

---

## 5. Incoming and Outgoing Anchors

Each tile can have:

- incoming anchor
- outgoing anchor

Example:

```text
        incoming
           ↓
      [ PHOTO ]
           ↓
        outgoing
```

The incoming anchor receives visual movement from the previous tile.

The outgoing anchor leads the eye toward the next tile.

For corner tiles, only the relevant directions need to be assigned.

---

## 6. Do Not Over-Connect

Not every boundary needs a dramatic connection.

A good composition may contain:

- several strong connections
- several supporting connections
- a few quiet boundaries

Quiet boundaries create breathing room.

Trying to make all 12 boundaries equally obvious can make the composition artificial and busy.

---

## 7. Avoid Artificial Bridges

Do not solve a weak connection by automatically adding:

- random lines
- arrows
- decorative shapes
- fake clouds
- invented objects
- excessive color overlays
- surreal elements unrelated to the photographs

The first solution should always be:

1. rearrange the photos
2. change the crop
3. change the visual direction
4. select a better photograph

Only then consider subtle editing.

---

## 8. Local Authenticity

When working with regional photography, connection rules must not override cultural or geographic reality.

For Western Sichuan and Tibetan plateau photography, possible authentic visual anchors include:

- snow mountains
- grassland
- rivers
- waterfalls
- roads
- prayer flags
- white stupas
- temples
- yaks
- Gesang flowers
- clouds
- rock formations

These are examples, not mandatory ingredients.

Never add a cultural symbol simply because it is stereotypically associated with a location.

---

## 9. Connection Priority

When choosing between two candidate arrangements, prefer this order:

1. strong direction + spatial continuity
2. strong shape continuity
3. strong object continuity
4. strong color continuity
5. strong texture continuity
6. narrative continuity
7. weak thematic similarity

This prevents the system from choosing photographs merely because they share a subject or color.

---

## 10. Final Connection Test

For every proposed 3×3 layout:

1. Check all 6 horizontal relationships.
2. Check all 6 vertical relationships.
3. Identify the strongest connections.
4. Identify the weakest connections.
5. Ask whether the weakest relationships can be improved by rearranging the nine images.
6. Check the whole grid at normal viewing size.
7. Check the whole grid at small/mobile size.

Final question:

> Does the eye travel across the grid naturally?

If yes, the connection system is working.

If no, revise the layout before adding decorative effects.
