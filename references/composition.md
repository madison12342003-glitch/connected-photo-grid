# Composition Rules

This document defines how to arrange the selected nine photographs into a coherent 3×3 composition.

The goal is not to maximize the beauty of individual tiles. The goal is to maximize the quality of the whole nine-image composition.

## 1. Think About the Whole Grid First

Use this layout:

    A B C
    D E F
    G H I

Treat it as one canvas divided into nine regions.

Before assigning individual photographs, identify:
- primary visual weight
- secondary visual weight
- main movement direction
- quiet areas
- strong edges
- likely center of attention
- possible entry point
- possible exit point

## 2. Visual Weight

Visual weight comes from:
- large subjects
- high contrast
- saturated colors
- dark areas
- bright areas
- faces
- animals
- architecture
- strong geometric shapes
- dense textures

Do not automatically place the most dramatic photograph in the center. The center should serve the composition.

## 3. The Center Tile

The center tile E touches four neighboring tiles: B, D, F, and H.

Therefore E should have high connection potential. A photograph with strong directional lines, flexible negative space, or multiple compatible visual anchors is often a better center candidate than an isolated spectacular image.

The center does not always need to be the strongest photograph.

## 4. Corners

Corner tiles have fewer direct neighbors:
- A → B, D
- C → B, F
- G → D, H
- I → F, H

Corners can carry distinctive photographs, unusual viewpoints, visual punctuation, cultural landmarks, or large areas of negative space.

Avoid putting four equally heavy images in all four corners.

## 5. Edges

Edge photographs control movement into and out of the composition.

Pay special attention to the shared boundaries of edge tiles. A useful edge can point toward the center, continue a mountain ridge, extend a road or river, or create a color bridge.

Avoid having too many important elements point outward from the grid.

## 6. Visual Flow

A composition should have at least one readable visual path.

Common conceptual patterns include:
- diagonal
- S-curve
- horizontal
- vertical

These are conceptual patterns, not rigid templates. Natural photographic lines should determine the actual flow.

## 7. Entry and Exit

A strong grid often has an entry point, a journey, and an exit point.

For example: road → valley → river → waterfall.

The entry and exit can be created through color, light, gaze, perspective, shape, or atmospheric depth.

## 8. Negative Space

Negative space is not wasted space.

Useful quiet areas can separate two dark images, two red elements, two visually dense scenes, or two strong subjects.

A grid that is connected everywhere but has no breathing room can feel noisy.

When the layout feels crowded, consider moving a photograph with more sky, water, grass, fog, or other quiet space into the composition.

## 9. Balance

Balance does not mean symmetry.

Distribute visual weight intentionally rather than creating rows or columns with identical visual intensity.

## 10. Avoid Subject Clustering

Do not automatically place similar subjects together.

Subject repetition should be purposeful. Repeated mountain forms, for example, are useful only when they create intentional visual continuity.

## 11. Crop Is Part of Composition

The best nine images may still fail if cropped badly.

When possible, evaluate horizontal crop, vertical crop, subject position, edge position, negative space, and direction of movement.

Do not crop away the connection anchor just to center the subject.

## 12. Arrangement Scoring

When several arrangements are possible, compare them conceptually using:

    Layout Score =
    connection quality
    + visual flow
    + weight balance
    + center quality
    + edge quality
    + color coherence
    + subject diversity
    + narrative coherence
    - visual conflict
    - unnecessary repetition

This does not require exact numerical computation. The purpose is to evaluate the whole layout instead of making decisions one photograph at a time.

## 13. Rearrangement Strategy

If the layout fails, do not immediately edit the images.

Try, in this order:
1. swap two neighboring photographs
2. swap a corner with an edge
3. change the center photograph
4. change the visual flow
5. change crops
6. reconsider the selected nine
7. only then consider image editing

Composition should solve composition problems.

## 14. Small-Screen Test

The final grid must work at small size.

At phone-screen size, ask:
- Can the overall flow still be seen?
- Is the center too dominant?
- Are the strongest colors distributed?
- Does one tile overpower the rest?
- Do neighboring images still feel related?

If the answer is no, revise the layout.

## 15. Final Composition Test

Before delivery, verify:

- Whole: does the nine-image grid feel like one artwork?
- Flow: can the eye travel through it naturally?
- Connections: do neighboring images have believable relationships?
- Balance: are visual weights distributed intentionally?
- Center: does the center connect the surrounding four tiles?
- Edges: do important elements avoid unnecessarily pointing out of the composition?
- Breathing room: are there enough quiet areas?
- Authenticity: does the composition still feel like real photography rather than a generic AI collage?

If the grid fails the whole-image test, do not optimize individual tiles further. Rework the composition first.
